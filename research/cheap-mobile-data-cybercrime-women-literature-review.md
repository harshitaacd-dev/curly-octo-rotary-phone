# Cheap Mobile Data and Crimes Against Women in India: Does the Internet Change How Often Women Are Victimised, or How Often They Report It?

## Abstract

In September 2016, Reliance Jio's entry into India's mobile market cut mobile data prices by an order of magnitude and set off a surge in internet use. Over the same years, police-recorded cybercrimes against women in India rose steeply. This review asks whether the published evidence can link the two. We searched along five angles (the economics of internet access and crime; mobile technology and intimate partner violence; reporting behaviour in Indian crime data; the Jio shock and women's connectivity in India; and measurement and policy on technology-facilitated violence) and verified 17 works. Three findings stand out. First, the best causal evidence on internet access and sexual crime comes from Europe and points in opposite directions: broadband raised reported sex crimes in Norway but did not raise rape in Germany. The Norwegian study's own framework allows more reporting, by itself, to produce the rise. Second, studies of mobile phones and intimate partner violence in developing countries mostly find lower violence where women are connected. These studies measure offline violence by partners, not cybercrime. Third, Indian police records of crimes against women respond strongly to how easy it is to report: two causal studies find recorded crime rising when reporting becomes easier while actual incidence stays flat. No retrieved study estimates the effect of cheap data, or of Jio, on cybercrime against women in India. Any study of that question has to separate more victimisation from more reporting. This review argues that this distinction decides what a finding means.

## 1. Introduction

Before Jio launched in September 2016, a gigabyte of mobile data in India cost a few hundred rupees. Within a few years it cost under twenty, and monthly data use rose several-fold within months. Police-recorded cyber offences against women (cyberstalking, publishing obscene material, morphing images, blackmail) rose sharply over the following years. The obvious hypothesis is that cheap data caused the rise. But the same period brought a national online portal for reporting cybercrime against women (2019), changes in how the National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) records these offences, and the COVID-19 lockdowns. Any of these could raise *recorded* crime without changing *actual* crime.

This review answers three research questions:

- **RQ1.** What does causal evidence show about how internet and mobile access affect crimes against women, both offline sexual crime and intimate partner violence?
- **RQ2.** Has the Jio data-price shock been used to study crime against women or cybercrime, and what do Indian connectivity studies show about women's access?
- **RQ3.** How can cybercrime against women in India be measured, what threatens identification, and what design is feasible for a short Master's project?

Section 2 describes the search. Section 3 lays out how the literature is organised. Sections 4 to 6 develop the evidence. Section 7 compares across the branches, Section 8 lists open problems, and Section 9 answers the research questions.

## 2. Methodology

**Search angles.** (a) Economics of internet and broadband access and crime; (b) mobile phones, 3G and intimate partner violence; (c) reporting behaviour in Indian crime statistics; (d) Jio and women's connectivity in India; (e) measurement, law and policy on technology-facilitated gender-based violence in India. The angles were run one after another by a single researcher.

**Main keyword groups.** broadband + sex crime; internet + rape; 3G / mobile phones + intimate partner violence; Jio + natural experiment / mobile data prices; NCRB + cyber crimes against women; women police stations / political representation + crime reporting; internet shutdowns + India; technology-facilitated gender-based violence + India.

**Time range.** 2006–2026.

**Inclusion.** Peer-reviewed articles, working papers in recognised series, and institutional reports whose existence, authors and main claim we confirmed. **Exclusion.** Journalism and exam-preparation sites are used only for dates and context. Works whose title, authors or year we could not confirm were dropped, including a journal article analysing cybercrime against women with NCRB data, an undergraduate thesis using Jio to study the digital gender gap, and a conference paper on rural roads and crime. Secondary sources disagree about NCRB cybercrime counts because category definitions changed, so we quote none.

**Final corpus.** 17 works.

**Evidence types used in this review:**

| Evidence type | What it is | Credibility for causal claims |
|---|---|---|
| Rollout designs (IV, difference-in-differences) | Variation in when or where broadband or 3G arrived | Strong if rollout timing is unrelated to underlying crime trends |
| Staggered policy rollouts | Variation in when reporting institutions or political reforms arrived | Strong for reporting effects |
| Randomised or programme evaluations | Phones or towers given to some women or villages but not others | Strong for women's access and use |
| Cross-sectional survey associations | Household surveys (e.g. the DHS) linking phone ownership and violence | Weak: who owns a phone is not random |
| State-level panel correlations | Internet diffusion across states over time | Weak to moderate |
| Content analysis and qualitative studies | Coded tweets; survivor interviews | Strong on what happens; cannot estimate effects |

## 3. How the literature is organised

We sort the literature by two dimensions: **the channel through which the internet acts** (it increases offending; it changes reporting; it protects women by raising their bargaining power or access to help) and **the outcome measured** (offline sexual crime; intimate partner violence; online abuse and cybercrime).

| | Offline sexual crime | Intimate partner violence | Online abuse / cybercrime |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Increases offending** | [1], [2] | [3] (attitudes, mixed) | [4] (descriptive) |
| **Changes reporting** | [1] (framework) | (none) | (none) |
| **Protects women** | [5] | [6], [7], [8], [3] | (none) |
| **Reporting effects in Indian crime data** | [9], [10] | [10] | (none) |

The online-abuse column is almost empty. That is the main finding of the taxonomy: we retrieved no causal study of internet access and cybercrime against women, in India or elsewhere. Two studies cut across the categories in useful ways. Bhuller et al. [1] measure offline sex crime but explicitly separate reporting, matching and offending channels. Amaral et al. [10] compare police records with survey reports of domestic violence, which lets them tell reporting apart from incidence.

## 4. Internet access and offline sexual crime: the direction depends on the setting (RQ1)

The two strongest European studies disagree. Bhuller, Havnes, Leuven and Mogstad [1] use the staggered rollout of broadband access points in Norway between 2000 and 2008 to estimate the effect of internet use. They find that internet use substantially increased reported rape and other sex crimes, with rises in charges and convictions as well. Their framework separates three channels: easier reporting, easier contact between potential offenders and victims, and a direct effect on the propensity to offend. Diegmann [2] studies the German broadband expansion with an instrument based on the local network's technical features. He finds no effect on rape and a *decline* in child sex abuse, which he reads as offenders substituting online material for offline offending. Kendall's earlier US state panel [5] went further: faster internet diffusion was associated with *less* rape, concentrated among young men. Because it relies on correlations across states, critics noted that early-adopting states might differ in other ways that also affect crime.

The disagreement has identifiable causes. Bhuller et al. [1] count *reported* crime in a country with high trust in the police, and their framework allows reporting to rise. The German [2] and US [5] studies cover different populations, periods and kinds of internet use, mainly fixed broadband at home. None of the three involves mobile data in a low-income setting, where phones are often shared and women's online presence is newer. For India, these studies show that either direction is possible, and that a change in reporting can produce an increase on its own.

## 5. Mobile phones and intimate partner violence: mostly protective, but only offline violence is measured (RQ1)

In developing countries, the evidence on intimate partner violence leans toward connection protecting women. Pesando [6] uses DHS data from ten low- and middle-income countries and finds that women's own phone ownership is associated with a 9–12% lower likelihood of recent emotional, physical or sexual violence. The proposed mechanisms are more say in household decisions and less acceptance of violence. Kashyap, Poddar, Koebe and Rotondi [7] use a stronger design, comparing areas before and after Nigeria's 3G rollout (difference-in-differences). They report reductions in emotional and physical violence, and in one version sexual violence, along with greater autonomy for women. For India, Dhamija, Ojha and Gupta [8] examine internet exposure and intimate partner violence; their title, "The Protective Power of Connectivity", indicates a protective finding, though we could not retrieve the details.

Attitudes are less clear-cut. Gillanders and McNamara [3] use Afrobarometer surveys and find that people in areas with more smartphone ownership are *more* likely to say wife-beating is never justified, but the relationship is statistically significant only among socially conservative respondents. Whether the effect works through social media is unclear: the association is similar whether people get news from social media or elsewhere.

**This branch has a scope limit for your question.** All of these studies measure *offline* violence by a partner. They show that connection can strengthen women's position at home. They say nothing about whether being online exposes women to stalking, image-based abuse or harassment by strangers. A finding that Jio raised cybercrime against women would not contradict them; the two could happen together.

| Study | Setting | Design | Outcome | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bhuller et al. [1] | Norway | Broadband rollout, IV | Reported sex crimes | Increase |
| Diegmann [2] | Germany | Broadband rollout, IV | Rape; child sex abuse | No effect; decrease |
| Kendall [5] | US states | Panel correlation | Rape | Decrease |
| Pesando [6] | 10 developing countries | Cross-sectional, matching, IV | Intimate partner violence | Decrease |
| Kashyap et al. [7] | Nigeria | 3G rollout, difference-in-differences | Intimate partner violence | Decrease |
| Gillanders & McNamara [3] | Africa | Cross-sectional | Attitudes to IPV | Less acceptance (conservatives only) |

*Table 1. The direction of the effect varies with the setting and the outcome. No row measures online abuse, and none is a causal study from India.*

## 6. India: reporting drives recorded crime, and the Jio shock has not been used for crime (RQ2, RQ3)

Two causal studies show that Indian police records of crimes against women move with how easy it is to report. Iyer, Mani, Mishra and Topalova [9] use the staggered introduction of quotas for women in local councils. Recorded crimes against women rose sharply, with no rise in crimes against men, and the authors attribute this mainly to more reporting rather than more crime. Amaral, Bhalotra and Prakash [10] use the staggered opening of women-only police stations. Recorded crimes against women rose by 29%, driven by domestic violence, with no change in femicide or in survey-reported domestic violence, which they also read as a reporting effect. Both studies use NCRB data, the same source a Jio-and-cybercrime study would rely on.

The reporting channel bears directly on cybercrime. India's National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal [11] opened in 2019 under the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre. It has a dedicated section for crimes against women and children and lets victims report anonymously, and an earlier version limited to sexual content began in 2018. Women police stations raised recorded crimes against women by about a quarter [10]. An anonymous online portal opened three years after Jio could plausibly raise recorded cybercrime against women by itself. A study that does not account for the portal cannot tell its effect apart from Jio's.

We found no study that uses the Jio shock to explain any crime outcome. The most relevant evidence on women's connectivity in India is a caution about what cheap access achieves. Barboni et al. [12] evaluate a state programme that gave free smartphones to women and built 4G towers in rural areas. The early gains were short-lived: nearly five years on, they find little lasting effect on phone use, gender norms or access to information, and the digital gender gap persisted. Many women lost control of their phones to male household members. For a Jio study, this means that cheaper data in a district does not imply that women there went online. The link from price to women's own use has to be shown, not assumed.

India's frequent internet shutdowns are documented by the Software Freedom Law Centre [13], which has tracked them since 2012 and classifies them by cause, duration and type. India ranks among the countries with the most shutdowns. We found no study using them to examine crime against women [unconfirmed: no work retrieved in this direction].

What is known about online abuse in India is descriptive. Amnesty International India's Troll Patrol [4] coded over 114,000 tweets sent to 95 women politicians around the 2019 election. It found abuse rates well above a comparable UK and US study, with Muslim and marginalised-caste women targeted more. Equality Now and Breakthrough [14] document survivors' experiences of doxxing, stalking and non-consensual sharing of intimate images, and describe police and platform responses as inadequate. These sources show what police counts miss: most incidents are never reported.

## 7. Comparing across the branches

**The same rise can mean different things.** Every causal study of crime against women in India that we retrieved concludes that more *recorded* crime reflected more reporting, not more crime [9], [10]. The European study that found the internet *raising* sex crime [1] also lists reporting as a possible channel. A finding that cheap data raised recorded cybercrime against women would therefore fit three stories: more abuse, more reporting (women who are online learn how to report and can do it online), or both. The literature offers two ways to separate them: compare police records with survey reports, as Amaral et al. do [10], or compare crimes against women with crimes against men, as Iyer et al. do [9].

**Protection at home, exposure online.** The research on intimate partner violence [6], [7], [8] points to connection protecting women. The descriptive work on online abuse [4], [14] points to connection exposing them. Both can hold, because they concern different offenders (partners versus strangers or acquaintances) and different spaces. A study measuring both outcomes in the same places would be the first to test whether cheap data shifts violence from offline to online.

**Jio's single launch date is a problem for identification.** The causal designs in this literature exploit staggered rollouts with fine-grained coverage maps: broadband in Norway and Germany [1], [2], 3G in Nigeria [7] and worldwide [16], mobile coverage across Africa [17], and internet outages [15]. Jio launched nationwide on one day, so its effect has to be identified from differences in *exposure* across places. Examples are pre-existing 4G coverage, smartphone ownership before 2016, or Jio's subscriber share by telecom circle. Barboni et al. [12] warn that exposure measured at the place level may not translate into women's own access.

## 8. Open problems and future directions

1. **Cheap data and cybercrime against women, separated from reporting.** This is the taxonomy's main empty cell. A design comparing states by their exposure to the price drop before 2016, and controlling for the 2019 portal [11], would give the first estimate. Placebo tests on cybercrime against men, or on cyber fraud, would help separate gendered abuse from a general rise in online crime.
2. **Do online and offline violence move together?** No study measures cyber abuse and intimate partner violence in the same places. India's National Family Health Surveys (NFHS-4 in 2015-16 and NFHS-5 in 2019-21) fall either side of Jio's launch and include domestic violence modules, and NCRB cybercrime data covers the same years. Combined at state level, they could test whether connection reduces partner violence while increasing cyber abuse, building on [8].
3. **Women's own phones versus the household's.** Pesando [6] finds effects for women's *own* phones, and Barboni et al. [12] show that women can lose control of phones given to them. Whether Jio raised women's own internet use, rather than their households', determines which channel could operate at all.
4. **Shutdowns as a reverse experiment.** Shutdowns [13] cut access abruptly in particular places. Whether recorded cyber offences against women fall during or after them would be a clean short-run test of the access channel.

## 9. Conclusion

**RQ1.** Causal evidence on internet access and offline sexual crime is mixed. It rose in Norway [1], with no effect on rape in Germany [2], and fell in a US correlation study [5]. For partner violence in developing countries, connection mostly protects women [6], [7], [8], though effects on attitudes vary [3]. None of this evidence measures cybercrime, and none is a causal estimate from India.

**RQ2.** We found no study using Jio to explain crime of any kind; the question is open. The best Indian evidence on women's connectivity [12] warns that cheaper or freer access does not reliably become women's own lasting internet use.

**RQ3.** The NCRB's annual "Crime in India" reports give state-level counts of cybercrimes against women. Category definitions have changed, and secondary sources' figures disagree, so use the original NCRB tables and document every definition. The main threats to identification are changes in reporting (the 2019 portal [11]; the reporting effects shown by [9] and [10]), COVID-19, and Jio's single national launch date. A design feasible in a short project would compare states by their exposure before 2016, before and after the launch, controlling for the portal and running placebo tests on crimes not targeted at women.

**This review's contribution** is to frame the question as more abuse versus more reporting. Without that distinction, a study would find a positive correlation between cheap data and cybercrime against women and could not say what it means. With it, the same data can answer a question no published study has addressed.
